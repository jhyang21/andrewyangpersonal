There's a particular kind of advice that sounds wise but feels wrong: focus on one thing. Pick a lane. Stop spreading yourself thin.

I tried that. For about a month in late 2025, I went heads-down on Relora and nothing else. No hackathons, no barista shifts, no random rabbit holes. Pure focus.

It was productive. I shipped faster. But something was off. I wasn't having new ideas. I wasn't making unexpected connections. I was optimizing, not discovering.

## The case for curiosity without a plan

Every meaningful opportunity in my life came from something I did without a clear reason:

- Working as a barista at Ape Coffee taught me more about daily human behavior than any user research study
- Competing in hackathons forced me to build things in hours, not months — a skill that completely changed how I prototype at Relora
- Doing metacognition research at Emory gave me a framework for thinking about how people think, which is the entire foundation of a personal CRM

None of these were strategic. All of them were essential.

## The contradiction I had to sit with

When I went full focus mode, I felt the pull of discipline. There's a whole genre of founder content about ruthless prioritization. And it's not wrong — you do need focus to ship.

But I was confusing focus with closure. I was shutting down the inputs that made my thinking interesting in the first place.

## Sidequests need a home base

The nuance I missed early on is that sidequests without an anchor become drift. You need something you're committed to — something that grounds the exploration and gives it somewhere to land.

For me, Relora is that anchor. The barista job, the hackathons, the tutoring — they're all sidequests that feed back into how I think about building Relora. Without the anchor, I'd just be wandering. With it, every sidequest has a return address.

## The real risk

The real risk isn't doing too many things. It's doing too few things and convincing yourself that's discipline. The most interesting founders I know have rich inner lives and diverse experiences. They're not optimizing a single metric — they're building a perspective.

Sidequests are underrated. But only if you have somewhere to bring what you find.
